#### Deck: GI::Anorectal Anatomy, Hemorrhoids & Fissure  (`GI_Anorectal_Anatomy_Hemorrhoids_Fissure.apkg`)

1. Q: What are the segments and distinctive features of the large intestine?  
   A:
   - Ileocecal valve → anus: cecum, ascending, transverse, descending, sigmoid colon, rectum
   - Taeniae coli (3 bands), haustra, appendices epiploicae — all end at rectosigmoid junction
   [p. 1] — media: GIANO_p1.png

2. Q: Describe the rectum's length, peritoneal covering and curves.  
   A:
   - 12–15 cm
   - Upper third: peritoneum anterior + lateral; middle: anterior only; lower: extraperitoneal
   - Three lateral curves (upper right, middle left, lower right) with folds
   [p. 1] — media: GIANO_p1.png

3. Q: What is the mesorectum, and what is TME?  
   A:
   - Fatty envelope around rectum: terminal IMA branches + regional lymphatics
   - TME: sharp dissection along avascular mesorectal fascia plane — gold standard in rectal cancer, cuts local recurrence
   [p. 1] — media: GIANO_p1.png

4. Q: What is the arterial supply of the colon and rectum?  
   A:
   - SMA (midgut: cecum → proximal 2/3 transverse): ileocolic, right colic, middle colic
   - IMA (hindgut: distal 1/3 transverse → upper rectum): left colic, sigmoid, superior rectal
   - Drummond's marginal artery links both
   [p. 2] — media: GIANO_p2.png

5. Q: How do the colon and rectum drain venous blood and lymph?  
   A:
   - SMV, IMV → portal; middle/inferior rectal veins → IVC, so low rectal cancer reaches lungs bypassing liver
   - Nodes follow arteries: epicolic → paracolic → intermediate → principal; high ligation needed
   [p. 2] — media: GIANO_p2.png

6. Q: What are the extent of the anal canal and the significance of the dentate line?  
   A:
   - Surgical canal 2.5–5 cm: anorectal ring → anal verge
   - Dentate (pectinate) line: junction of endoderm (hindgut) and ectoderm (proctodeum)
   [p. 2] — media: GIANO_p2.png

7. Q: How do epithelium, nerves and lymph differ above vs below the dentate line?  
   A:
   - Above: columnar; visceral (inferior hypogastric plexus), painless; internal iliac nodes
   - Below: non-keratinised squamous to Hilton's line; somatic (pudendal/inferior rectal), painful; superficial inguinal nodes
   [p. 2] — media: GIANO_p2.png

8. Q: How do internal and external hemorrhoids differ?  
   A:
   - Internal: above dentate line, superior plexus/rectal vessels → portal; mucosa; painless bright red bleeding, prolapse
   - External: below, inferior plexus, middle/inferior rectal vessels → IVC; anoderm; painful if thrombosed, hygiene difficulty
   [pp. 2, 3, 7] — media: GIANO_p2.png, GIANO_p3.png, GIANO_p7.png

9. Q: Compare the internal and external anal sphincters.  
   A:
   - IAS: thickened circular smooth muscle of rectum; involuntary autonomic; 50–70% of resting tone
   - EAS: striated, voluntary somatic; subcutaneous, superficial and deep loops
   [p. 3] — media: GIANO_p3.png

10. Q: What is the anorectal ring and what forms it?  
   A:
   - Junction of rectum and anal canal
   - Puborectalis, deep external sphincter, conjoined longitudinal muscle, highest part of internal sphincter
   [p. 3] — media: GIANO_p3.png

11. Q: What are the anal cushions?  
   A:
   - Normal fibrovascular cushions: left lateral, right anterior, right posterior (3, 7, 11 o'clock, lithotomy)
   - Arterial + venous blood; aid continence, air-tightness, mucus
   - Engorgement = hemorrhoidal disease (piles)
   [pp. 3, 7, 8] — media: GIANO_p3.png, GIANO_p7.png, GIANO_p8.png

12. Q: What are the parts and nerve supply of levator ani?  
   A:
   - Pubococcygeus (pubovisceral), puborectalis, iliococcygeus; supports pelvic organs with sphincters, perineal body
   - Pudendal branches (perineal, inferior rectal) + direct S3/S4 (levator ani nerve)
   [p. 4] — media: GIANO_p4.png

13. Q: Why do the anorectal spaces matter, and what are they?  
   A:
   - Myofascial spaces that channel spread of anorectal sepsis
   - Perianal, intersphincteric, ischioanal, supralevator
   - Pudendal (Alcock's) canal lies in lateral wall
   [p. 4] — media: GIANO_p4.png

14. Q: What do the sympathetic and parasympathetic supplies do to the rectum and anus?  
   A:
   - Sympathetic (L1–L3, superior hypogastric plexus): inhibits peristalsis, contracts IAS, ejaculation
   - Parasympathetic (S2–S4, nervi erigentes): stimulates peristalsis, relaxes IAS, erection
   [p. 5] — media: GIANO_p5.png

15. Q: What does the pudendal nerve supply, and why does pelvic nerve anatomy matter?  
   A:
   - Pudendal (S2–S4): voluntary motor to EAS; sensation to perianal skin and canal below dentate line
   - Iatrogenic injury in pelvic dissection (e.g. TME) → devastating functional loss
   [p. 5] — media: GIANO_p5.png

16. Q: How does the colon handle fluid and electrolytes?  
   A:
   - Receives ~1500 mL/day, absorbs all but ~100–200 mL
   - Absorbs Na⁺, Cl⁻, water; secretes K⁺, HCO₃⁻ → severe diarrhea causes hypokalemic metabolic acidosis
   [p. 5] — media: GIANO_p5.png

17. Q: Describe colonic motility and the role of colonic bacteria.  
   A:
   - Segmental contractions: mixing, absorption
   - HAPCs (mass movements): propulsion, on waking/eating (gastrocolic reflex)
   - Bacteria ferment carbohydrate → SCFAs; butyrate fuels colonocytes
   [p. 5] — media: GIANO_p5.png

18. Q: What are the steps of defecation?  
   A:
   - Rectal filling: mass movements stretch rectum
   - RAIR: distension → involuntary IAS relaxation
   - Sampling: transition zone tells gas/liquid/solid
   - Evacuation: relax EAS + puborectalis, angle straightens, Valsalva, rectum contracts
   [pp. 5, 6] — media: GIANO_p5.png, GIANO_p6.png

19. Q: How do the IAS and EAS contribute to continence, and what follows their failure?  
   A:
   - IAS: resting tone → passive incontinence (leakage without awareness)
   - EAS: voluntary squeeze → urge incontinence (can't hold when urge hits)
   [p. 6] — media: GIANO_p6.png

20. Q: How do puborectalis and rectal compliance contribute to continence?  
   A:
   - Puborectalis: U-sling, anorectal angle 90–100° (flap valve) → solid incontinence, pelvic floor descent
   - Compliance: low-pressure storage → urgency, frequency (post-radiation proctitis)
   [p. 6] — media: GIANO_p6.png

21. Q: How are internal hemorrhoids graded?  
   A:
   - By history, not examination
   - I: bleeding, no prolapse
   - II: prolapse, reduces spontaneously
   - III: needs manual reduction
   - IV: permanently prolapsed
   [p. 7] — media: GIANO_p7.png

22. Q: What causes hemorrhoidal bleeding and prolapse?  
   A:
   - Venous obstruction theory: straining blocks cushion drainage → engorged, thin, friable mucosa bleeds at defecation
   - Sliding theory: straining/hard stool disrupt elastic fibres anchoring cushions to IAS → prolapse
   [p. 8] — media: GIANO_p8.png

23. Q: What are the characteristics of hemorrhoidal bleeding and prolapse?  
   A:
   - Bleeding: with/after stool, bright red, drips into pan, not mixed, dark or melena; rarely anemia
   - Prolapse: straining, walking, lifting; fullness that resolves on reduction
   [p. 8] — media: GIANO_p8.png

24. Q: What predisposes to piles, and what are their main symptoms?  
   A:
   - ↑ intra-abdominal pressure: obesity, constipation, pregnancy
   - Bright red bleeding, prolapsing mass, mucoid discharge
   - Painless unless external thrombosed or prolapsed; thrombosis resolves or leaves itchy tag
   [p. 8] — media: GIANO_p8.png

25. Q: When is rigid sigmoidoscopy vs colonoscopy used for per-anal bleeding?  
   A:
   - Rigid sigmoidoscopy: young, bleeding with hemorrhoids, no systemic symptoms or CRC family history
   - Colonoscopy: older, CRC family history or changed bowel habit
   [p. 9] — media: GIANO_p9.png

26. Q: What are the treatment categories for hemorrhoids, and which lifestyle measures help?  
   A:
   - Lifestyle/diet → office procedures (grade I–II) → hemorrhoidectomy (III–IV)
   - More fluid and fibre, avoid prolonged straining, stool modifiers, sitz baths/creams (prolonged topical steroids harmful)
   [p. 9] — media: GIANO_p9.png

27. Q: How do rubber band ligation and infrared coagulation work?  
   A:
   - RBL (grade I–II, some III): band strangles redundant mucosa above cushion; ulcer heals, fixing mucosa to muscle
   - Infrared coagulation (I–II): repeated sessions; RBL often better
   [p. 9] — media: GIANO_p9.png

28. Q: How does sclerotherapy work and which agents are used?  
   A:
   - Submucosal irritant → ↓ vascularity, ↑ fibrosis, fixes bundle to IAS → less bleeding and prolapse
   - Phenol in oil or sodium morrhuate
   [p. 9] — media: GIANO_p9.png

29. Q: What is the principle of hemorrhoidectomy, and how do open and closed techniques differ?  
   A:
   - Reduce blood flow, remove redundant tissue
   - Open (Milligan–Morgan, commonest): ligate, excise, leave open; 1–3 piles
   - Closed (Ferguson): suture-ligate pedicle, close wound; minimal anoderm or pain, stenosis
   [p. 10] — media: GIANO_p10.png

30. Q: What are the main complications of open/closed hemorrhoidectomy?  
   A:
   - Severe pain (avoid intra-anal packing)
   - Urinary retention (conservative, then catheter)
   - Reactionary/secondary hemorrhage (readmit, control)
   - Anal stenosis (leave mucosal islands between wounds)
   [p. 10] — media: GIANO_p10.png

31. Q: What are the advantages of diathermy/LigaSure and stapled hemorrhoidectomy?  
   A:
   - Diathermy/LigaSure: less bleeding and pain, no pack
   - Stapled: transanal circular stapling of redundant mucosa; no anoderm cut → less pain, shorter stay, early return; similar complications
   [pp. 10, 11] — media: GIANO_p10.png, GIANO_p11.png

32. Q: What are pile sutures and ligation anopexy?  
   A:
   - Pile sutures: three-level ligation of perforating veins at cushion base
   - Ligation anopexy: reduces mucosa and blood flow, repositions cushions, fixes them to IAS
   [p. 11] — media: GIANO_p11.png

33. Q: What is an anal fissure, where does it occur, and what causes it?  
   A:
   - Elongated ulcer in long axis of anal canal
   - Midline posterior, midline anterior, lateral
   - High anal pressure, trauma/ischemia, IBD, post-operative stenosis
   [p. 12] — media: GIANO_p12.png

34. Q: How do acute and chronic anal fissures differ pathologically?  
   A:
   - Acute: deep tear of anal margin skin to dentate line; edematous edges; sphincter spasm
   - Chronic: indurated edges, scar base, sentinel pile, fibrosed muscle; may have specific cause (Crohn's)
   [p. 13] — media: GIANO_p13.png

35. Q: How does an anal fissure present and how is it treated?  
   A:
   - Painful defecation, bright red bleeding, constipation, puckered anus
   - Glyceryl trinitrate, laxatives, Xylocaine ointment
   - Surgery rarely for acute; chronic → lateral or dorsal sphincterotomy
   [p. 13] — media: GIANO_p13.png
