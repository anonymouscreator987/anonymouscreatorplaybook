#### Deck: REPRO::Session 01 – Breast Anatomy and Investigations  (`REPRO_S01_Breast_Anatomy_Investigations.apkg`)

1. Q: How does the breast develop embryologically, and where do accessory nipples occur?  
   A:
   - Ectodermal mammary ridge (milk line), axilla → groin, appears week 5–6
   - Regresses except pectoral region (4th intercostal space)
   - Failed regression → accessory nipples/breast tissue along milk line
   [pp. 1, 3, 17] — media: BRA01_p1.png, BRA01_p3.png, BRA01_p17.png

2. Q: Define amastia, amazia, athelia and symmastia; what causes iatrogenic hypoplasia?  
   A:
   - Amastia: no breast (ridge arrest, week 6)
   - Amazia: no breast tissue, nipple present
   - Athelia: no nipple
   - Symmastia: webbing across midline
   - Iatrogenic hypoplasia: prepubertal trauma, infection, radiotherapy or bud biopsy
   [p. 2] — media: BRA01_p2.png

3. Q: What are the features and treatment of Poland syndrome?  
   A:
   - Breast hypoplasia or absence
   - Rib/costal cartilage defects; absent or hypoplastic pectoralis major (sternocostal part)
   - Brachysyndactyly (short, webbed fingers)
   - Treat: reconstruction — implants, fat grafting, latissimus dorsi flap
   [pp. 2, 3, 17] — media: BRA01_p2.png, BRA01_p3.png, BRA01_p17.png

4. Q: Describe polythelia, polymastia and accessory axillary breast tissue.  
   A:
   - Polythelia: milk line, never below umbilicus; commonest just below breast
   - Polymastia: anywhere along milk line
   - Axillary tissue: usually bilateral, enlarges in pregnancy/lactation, can develop any breast disease incl. cancer
   [pp. 3, 17] — media: BRA01_p3.png, BRA01_p17.png

5. Q: What is congenital inverted nipple, and which features suggest malignant inversion instead?  
   A:
   - Failure of nipple pit to evert above skin
   - Causes breastfeeding difficulty
   - Suspect cancer: recent, unilateral, fixed, with lump or discharge
   [pp. 3, 4] — media: BRA01_p3.png, BRA01_p4.png

6. Q: Where does the breast lie, and what forms its bed?  
   A:
   - Modified sweat gland, superficial fascia; 2nd–6th rib, sternal edge → mid-axillary line
   - NAC: 4th intercostal space (nulliparous)
   - Bed: pectoralis major (2/3), serratus anterior (1/3), external oblique, rectus sheath
   [pp. 4, 6] — media: BRA01_p4.png, BRA01_p6.png

7. Q: What is the axillary tail, and how is the breast divided into regions?  
   A:
   - Tail of Spence: through foramen of Langer (deep fascia gap) into axilla; mimics axillary mass
   - Four quadrants + central (subareolar) + axillary tail
   - Upper outer quadrant: most glandular tissue, commonest cancer site
   [p. 4] — media: BRA01_p4.png

8. Q: What tissues make up the breast and how is the parenchyma organised?  
   A:
   - Glandular epithelium (luminal cells + outer myoepithelial layer), fibrous stroma, fat
   - 15–20 lobes, each drained by one lactiferous duct
   - Lobe → lobules → alveoli (acini)
   [p. 5] — media: BRA01_p5.png

9. Q: How do the ducts end, what is the TDLU, and how does duct disease present?  
   A:
   - Ducts open separately on nipple, dilating into lactiferous sinus just before
   - TDLU (terminal ducts + acini): functional unit, origin of most cancers
   - Duct infiltration → nipple retraction; duct pathology → discharge
   [pp. 5, 17] — media: BRA01_p5.png, BRA01_p17.png

10. Q: What are Cooper's ligaments and their clinical significance?  
   A:
   - Fibrous bands from superficial fascia through parenchyma to deep fascia (skin → deep fascia)
   - Cancer tethering → dimpling; blocked dermal lymphatics → peau d'orange
   - Ageing stretch → ptosis
   [pp. 5, 6, 17] — media: BRA01_p5.png, BRA01_p6.png, BRA01_p17.png

11. Q: What is the arterial supply of the breast?  
   A:
   - Internal mammary perforators (~60%): medial/central, 2nd–5th spaces
   - Lateral thoracic (~30%): upper outer
   - Others: thoracoacromial pectoral branches, posterior intercostal lateral branches (3rd–5th), thoracodorsal
   [pp. 7, 17] — media: BRA01_p7.png, BRA01_p17.png

12. Q: Why does breast blood supply matter surgically?  
   A:
   - Dictates pedicle choice in reduction mammoplasty (e.g. superomedial pedicle on internal mammary perforators)
   - Nipple blood supply must be preserved
   - Perforator knowledge keeps conservative-mastectomy skin flaps and perforator flaps viable
   [p. 7] — media: BRA01_p7.png

13. Q: Describe the venous drainage of the breast and Batson's plexus.  
   A:
   - Superficial (subdermal): relevant to reduction de-epithelialisation
   - Deep follows arteries: internal thoracic, posterior intercostal perforators, axillary vein (main)
   - Batson's plexus (skull base–sacrum) via intercostals → vertebral, skull, pelvic, CNS metastases
   [pp. 7, 8, 17] — media: BRA01_p7.png, BRA01_p8.png, BRA01_p17.png

14. Q: What is the sensory nerve supply of the breast and NAC?  
   A:
   - Anterior and lateral cutaneous branches of 2nd–5th intercostal nerves
   - NAC: mainly lateral cutaneous branch of 4th intercostal nerve
   - Preserve nipple innervation, especially in aesthetic surgery
   [pp. 8, 17] — media: BRA01_p8.png, BRA01_p17.png

15. Q: Which nerves are at risk in axillary surgery, and what does injury cause?  
   A:
   - Long thoracic (serratus anterior) → winged scapula
   - Thoracodorsal (latissimus dorsi; LD flap) → weak adduction/internal rotation
   - Medial/lateral pectoral → pectoral atrophy
   - Intercostobrachial (axilla, inner arm) → numbness
   [pp. 9, 17] — media: BRA01_p9.png, BRA01_p17.png

16. Q: Where does breast lymph drain, including alternative routes?  
   A:
   - ~75% axillary, 20–25% internal mammary (medial breast), little posterior intercostal; via subareolar plexus of Sappey
   - Blocked: opposite breast, abdomen (subdiaphragmatic, liver), supraclavicular
   - Follows blood supply → rib/lung metastases
   [pp. 9, 17] — media: BRA01_p9.png, BRA01_p17.png

17. Q: Name and locate the six anatomical groups of axillary nodes.  
   A:
   - Lateral: along axillary vein; drains arm
   - Anterior: lower pectoralis minor border; main breast drainage
   - Posterior: subscapular vessels
   - Central: behind pectoralis minor
   - Apical: above pectoralis minor; receives all
   - Rotter's: between pectorals
   [p. 10] — media: BRA01_p10.png

18. Q: What are the surgical levels of axillary nodes and why do they matter?  
   A:
   - Pectoralis minor: I lateral (anterior, posterior, lateral); II behind (central, Rotter's); III medial (apical)
   - Level I: first involved, ~80% of nodes
   - Staging: SLNB if clinically N0; clearance if node-positive
   [pp. 10, 11, 17] — media: BRA01_p10.png, BRA01_p11.png, BRA01_p17.png

19. Q: What do the breast hormones do, and how do cycle and menopause change the breast?  
   A:
   - Oestrogen: ducts; progesterone: lobules; prolactin: milk; oxytocin: let-down
   - Luteal swelling → cyclical mastalgia; assess week after menses
   - Menopause: fat replaces glands → mammography more sensitive
   [p. 11] — media: BRA01_p11.png

20. Q: What should a breast history cover?  
   A:
   - Lump (duration, cyclical change), pain, discharge (colour, single/multiple ducts), skin/nipple change
   - Menstrual/obstetric history, hormone use, family history (breast/ovarian), previous breast disease
   [p. 11] — media: BRA01_p11.png

21. Q: How is the breast inspected?  
   A:
   - Sitting: arms by sides → raised above head → hands pressed on hips
   - Size, symmetry, contour, visible lumps
   - Skin: dimpling, peau d'orange, erythema, ulceration
   - Nipple: retraction, deviation, eczema, discharge
   [p. 11] — media: BRA01_p11.png

22. Q: How is the breast palpated?  
   A:
   - Supine, ipsilateral arm behind head; flat of fingers
   - All quadrants, axillary tail, subareolar region — concentric circles, radial spokes or vertical strips
   - Then axilla, supraclavicular fossae, opposite breast
   [pp. 11, 12] — media: BRA01_p11.png, BRA01_p12.png

23. Q: How do you describe a breast lump?  
   A:
   - Site: quadrant, clock position, distance from nipple
   - Size, shape, consistency, surface, edge
   - Mobility, fixity to skin/muscle, tenderness
   [pp. 11, 12] — media: BRA01_p11.png, BRA01_p12.png

24. Q: What is triple assessment?  
   A:
   - Clinical: history + examination
   - Imaging: ultrasound <35–40, mammography ≥35–40
   - Pathology: image-guided core biopsy (preferred) or FNAC
   - Benign only if all concordant; discordant → repeat biopsy/excision
   [pp. 12, 17] — media: BRA01_p12.png, BRA01_p17.png

25. Q: What is mammography, and what are its views and limitations?  
   A:
   - Low-dose X-ray; first test for symptomatic breast >35
   - Two views each breast: MLO (45°), craniocaudal
   - Unreliable <35 and lactating (dense)
   - Tomosynthesis (3D): better in dense breasts, fewer recalls
   [pp. 12, 13] — media: BRA01_p12.png, BRA01_p13.png

26. Q: What are the indications for mammography?  
   A:
   - Screening: USPSTF (2024) every 2 years, 40–74; NHS every 3 years, 50–71; high-risk earlier, often annual MRI
   - Diagnostic: symptoms or abnormal screen
   - Follow-up: post-BCS, opposite breast
   [p. 13] — media: BRA01_p13.png

27. Q: What mammographic features suggest benign vs malignant?  
   A:
   - Benign: round/oval, circumscribed, fat-containing, coarse 'popcorn' or scattered round calcification
   - Malignant: irregular, spiculated/ill-defined, distortion without prior surgery, microcalcifications <0.5 mm — clustered, linear, branching, pleomorphic, casting
   [pp. 13, 14] — media: BRA01_p13.png, BRA01_p14.png

28. Q: What are BI-RADS 0–3 and their management?  
   A:
   - 0: incomplete → recall/prior films
   - 1 negative, 2 benign → routine screening (essentially 0%)
   - 3: probably benign (>0–≤2%) → 6-month follow-up
   [pp. 15, 17] — media: BRA01_p15.png, BRA01_p17.png

29. Q: What are BI-RADS 4–6 and how is density graded?  
   A:
   - 4: suspicious → biopsy (4a >2–10%, 4b >10–50%, 4c >50–<95%)
   - 5: ≥95% → biopsy
   - 6: proven malignancy → excision
   - Density A (fatty) – D (extremely dense)
   [pp. 15, 17] — media: BRA01_p15.png, BRA01_p17.png

30. Q: What are the uses of breast ultrasound?  
   A:
   - First choice <35, pregnancy, lactation; no radiation
   - Cystic vs solid; Doppler flow helps benign vs malignant
   - Best for axillary nodes
   - Guides aspiration, core biopsy, clip placement
   [p. 15] — media: BRA01_p15.png

31. Q: Which ultrasound features suggest benign vs malignant?  
   A:
   - Benign: wider than tall (parallel), circumscribed, homogeneous/hyperechoic, posterior enhancement (cysts)
   - Malignant: taller than wide, spiculated/angular/microlobulated, heterogeneous/hypoechoic, acoustic shadowing
   [pp. 15, 16] — media: BRA01_p15.png, BRA01_p16.png

32. Q: What are the indications and limitations of breast MRI?  
   A:
   - Contrast-enhanced; most sensitive test
   - Uses: high-risk screening (BRCA, chest radiotherapy), lobular/multifocal cancer, occult primary, neoadjuvant response
   - Limits: false positives, cost, contrast
   [pp. 16, 17] — media: BRA01_p16.png, BRA01_p17.png

33. Q: Compare FNAC, core, vacuum-assisted and excision biopsy.  
   A:
   - FNAC: quick, cheap, cells only — can't separate in situ/invasive; cysts, nodes
   - Core (14G, US/stereotactic): standard; histology, grade, ER/PR/HER2
   - Vacuum: microcalcifications, small benign lesions
   - Excision: discordant/inconclusive
   [pp. 16, 17] — media: BRA01_p16.png, BRA01_p17.png

#### Deck: REPRO::Session 02 – Benign Breast Diseases  (`REPRO_S02_Benign_Breast_Diseases.apkg`)

34. Q: How are benign breast conditions classified by development and involution?  
   A:
   - Congenital; inflammatory (mastitis, abscess, granulomatous mastitis)
   - Developmental: fibroadenoma, hamartoma; cyclical: fibrocystic change, cyclic mastalgia
   - Involutional: duct ectasia, sclerosing adenosis; miscellaneous: fat necrosis, Mondor's, galactocele
   [p. 1] — media: BBD02_p1.png

35. Q: What is ANDI?  
   A:
   - Aberrations of Normal Development and Involution: benign, non-pathological variation
   - Exaggerated development → lumps/nodularity; irregular involution → cysts, fibrosis, lumpiness
   - Not true diseases; histology instead grades epithelial proliferation/atypia
   [pp. 1, 3, 17] — media: BBD02_p1.png, BBD02_p3.png, BBD02_p17.png

36. Q: ANDI, early reproductive phase (15–25): normal → aberration → disease?  
   A:
   - Lobular development → fibroadenoma → giant/multiple fibroadenomas
   - Stromal development → adolescent hypertrophy → gigantomastia
   - Nipple eversion → inversion → subareolar abscess, duct fistula
   [p. 2] — media: BBD02_p2.png

37. Q: ANDI, later reproductive (25–40) and involution (35–55) phases: normal → aberration → disease?  
   A:
   - Cyclical change → mastalgia/nodularity → incapacitating mastalgia
   - Pregnancy hyperplasia → bloody discharge
   - Lobular involution → macrocysts, sclerosing lesions
   - Duct involution → ectasia → periductal mastitis
   - Epithelial turnover → hyperplasia → atypia
   [p. 2] — media: BBD02_p2.png

38. Q: Histological classification of benign lesions: categories, examples and cancer risk?  
   A:
   - Non-proliferative (≈1): cysts, apocrine change, calcifications, mild hyperplasia
   - Proliferative (1.5–2×): sclerosing adenosis, radial scar, ductal hyperplasia, papilloma, fibroadenoma
   - Proliferative + atypia (4–5×, higher with family history): ADH, ALH
   [pp. 2, 17] — media: BBD02_p2.png, BBD02_p17.png

39. Q: How is duct epithelial hyperplasia graded, and why is ADH on core biopsy excised?  
   A:
   - Mild 3–4 cell layers; moderate ≥5
   - Florid: ≥70% of minor duct lumen, solid/papillary, increased risk
   - ADH excised: coexisting DCIS or invasive cancer often found
   [p. 3] — media: BBD02_p3.png

40. Q: Which organisms cause breast infection?  
   A:
   - Neonatal, skin, lactational: S. aureus (lactational rarely S. epidermidis, streptococci)
   - Non-lactational: S. aureus, enterococci, anaerobes, streptococci
   [p. 3] — media: BBD02_p3.png

41. Q: What are the guiding principles in breast infection?  
   A:
   - Mostly women 18–50: lactational/non-lactational, skin or tissue
   - Antibiotics early to prevent abscess
   - Fails one course → suspect abscess → ultrasound
   - Non-settling inflammation, especially non-lactating → triple assessment to exclude inflammatory cancer
   [p. 3] — media: BBD02_p3.png

42. Q: Describe mastitis neonatorum and primary/secondary skin infection of the breast.  
   A:
   - Bud enlarges weeks 1–2 (maternal hormones), may reach several cm; S. aureus infection; antibiotics; collection → peripheral incision protecting bud
   - Skin: primary (cellulitis, furuncle) or secondary (sebaceous cyst, hidradenitis suppurativa)
   [p. 4] — media: BBD02_p4.png

43. Q: What are the features of acute lactational mastitis and where can abscesses form?  
   A:
   - Early weeks (first 6) or weaning; commonest after first pregnancy
   - Milk stasis, nipple cracking; S. aureus
   - Point tenderness, erythema, fever
   - Abscess: subcutaneous, subareolar, interlobular, retromammary; uni/multicentric
   [pp. 4, 17] — media: BBD02_p4.png, BBD02_p17.png

44. Q: How is lactational mastitis managed?  
   A:
   - Analgesia/NSAIDs, rest, cold packs; continue breastfeeding/expressing (drains segment)
   - Flucloxacillin or co-amoxiclav; clarithromycin if allergic — all breastfeeding-safe
   - Ultrasound to detect abscess
   [pp. 5, 17] — media: BBD02_p5.png, BBD02_p17.png

45. Q: How is a lactational breast abscess drained?  
   A:
   - Repeated US-guided aspiration + oral antibiotics every 2–3 days until no pus
   - I&D if skin thinned/necrotic or aspiration fails: circumareolar or Langer's-line incision; culture pus
   [pp. 5, 17] — media: BBD02_p5.png, BBD02_p17.png

46. Q: What causes chronic and peripheral non-lactational abscesses?  
   A:
   - Chronic: poor drainage or injudicious antibiotics; thick fibrous wall mimics carcinoma → biopsy wall
   - Peripheral: diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, steroids, trauma; S. aureus
   [p. 6] — media: BBD02_p6.png

47. Q: What are the features of central (periareolar) infection / periductal mastitis?  
   A:
   - Young (~30) smokers; smoking damages subareolar ducts → aerobic + anaerobic infection
   - Recurrent periareolar inflammation ± mass, abscess; fistula if recurrent
   - Subtle retraction; purulent discharge
   [pp. 6, 13, 17] — media: BBD02_p6.png, BBD02_p13.png, BBD02_p17.png

48. Q: How are periareolar infection and periductal mastitis treated?  
   A:
   - Stop smoking (cuts recurrence)
   - Aerobic + anaerobic antibiotics; US-guided aspiration of abscess
   - Recurrence/fistula → excise fistula tract and involved ducts (total duct excision)
   [pp. 6, 13, 17] — media: BBD02_p6.png, BBD02_p13.png, BBD02_p17.png

49. Q: What is idiopathic granulomatous mastitis and how does it present?  
   A:
   - Non-caseating granulomas centred on lobules ± microabscesses
   - Childbearing age, within years of pregnancy
   - Firm mass mimicking cancer; pain, skin inflammation, ulceration, abscesses/sinuses
   [p. 6] — media: BBD02_p6.png

50. Q: What causes IGM and how is it diagnosed?  
   A:
   - Unknown; autoimmune, Corynebacterium kroppenstedtii, hyperprolactinaemia, pregnancy linked
   - Exclude TB/infection first
   - Ultrasound ± mammography; core biopsy essential (mimics cancer)
   [pp. 6, 17] — media: BBD02_p6.png, BBD02_p17.png

51. Q: How is IGM managed medically?  
   A:
   - Mild: observe (may resolve)
   - Moderate/severe: steroids after excluding infection (systemic; intralesional/topical if localised)
   - Steroid-resistant/dependent: methotrexate
   - Antibiotics only if infected, culture-guided (specific for Corynebacterium)
   [pp. 7, 17] — media: BBD02_p7.png, BBD02_p17.png

52. Q: What is the role of intervention in IGM?  
   A:
   - Abscess: US-guided aspiration/drainage
   - Refractory localised: wide local excision
   - Avoid aggressive first-line surgery: poor healing, deformity, recurrence
   [pp. 7, 17] — media: BBD02_p7.png, BBD02_p17.png

53. Q: What are the features of fibroadenoma?  
   A:
   - Commonest benign female breast tumour; stroma + epithelium
   - Age 15–25; grows to 1–2 cm (<3 cm), then stable
   - Smooth, firm, rubbery, painless, highly mobile 'breast mouse'
   [pp. 7, 9, 17] — media: BBD02_p7.png, BBD02_p9.png, BBD02_p17.png

54. Q: What are the variants of fibroadenoma?  
   A:
   - Giant: >5 cm
   - Juvenile: adolescents, rapid growth
   - Complex: cysts, sclerosing adenosis or calcification — slightly raised cancer risk
   - Multiple: avoid excising concordant benign lesions
   [p. 7] — media: BBD02_p7.png

55. Q: How is fibroadenoma managed?  
   A:
   - Triple assessment; typical, asymptomatic, concordant → observe
   - Excise (extracapsular; rim if phyllodes suspected): rapid growth, >4–6 cm, discordance, atypia/phyllodes not excluded
   - Vacuum excision for small lesions
   [pp. 7, 8, 17] — media: BBD02_p7.png, BBD02_p8.png, BBD02_p17.png

56. Q: What is phyllodes tumour pathologically?  
   A:
   - Proliferating ducts + cellular stroma; leaf-like clefts
   - Age 35–50 (10–15 years older than fibroadenoma)
   - Benign (commonest), borderline, malignant; malignancy: stromal cellularity, mitoses, anaplasia, stromal overgrowth
   [pp. 8, 9, 17] — media: BBD02_p8.png, BBD02_p9.png, BBD02_p17.png

57. Q: How does phyllodes present, spread and get diagnosed?  
   A:
   - Rapid, large, smooth, lobulated; shiny stretched skin, dilated veins; ulceration without infiltration
   - Haematogenous metastasis (lungs, skeleton, heart, liver) → no axillary surgery
   - Core biopsy, not FNAC
   [pp. 8, 17] — media: BBD02_p8.png, BBD02_p17.png

58. Q: How is phyllodes tumour treated?  
   A:
   - Excision with ≥1 cm margin; recurrence common if inadequate, unpredictable
   - Mastectomy: massive, recurrent or malignant
   - Radiotherapy: malignant with close margins
   [pp. 9, 17] — media: BBD02_p9.png, BBD02_p17.png

59. Q: What is intraductal papilloma and how do solitary and multiple types differ?  
   A:
   - Epithelium on fibrovascular core within major duct
   - Solitary central: 35–55; single-duct serous/bloody discharge ± subareolar mass; benign without atypia
   - Multiple peripheral: TDLU, younger, less discharge, more atypia/malignancy
   [pp. 10, 17] — media: BBD02_p10.png, BBD02_p17.png

60. Q: How is intraductal papilloma investigated and managed?  
   A:
   - Mammography/tomosynthesis, targeted US; core biopsy visible lesions; MRI if negative but suspicious
   - Persistent discharge → microdochectomy (total duct excision if duct unidentified or older)
   - Excise if atypia/discordant
   [pp. 10, 16, 17] — media: BBD02_p10.png, BBD02_p16.png, BBD02_p17.png

61. Q: What is mastalgia and how is it classified?  
   A:
   - Commonest breast clinic complaint; luteal swelling; severe → ↑LH/FSH (?prolactin, low fatty acids); rarely cancer
   - Cyclical: bilateral, heavy, upper outer, relieved by menses
   - Non-cyclical: breast or chest wall
   [p. 10] — media: BBD02_p10.png

62. Q: How is mastalgia evaluated?  
   A:
   - Pain chart over 2–3 cycles
   - Gentle exam: exclude lump, find chest-wall origin; nodularity common, unrelated to severity
   - No imaging without lump; mammography >35–40 for focal non-cyclical pain
   [pp. 10, 11] — media: BBD02_p10.png, BBD02_p11.png

63. Q: How is mastalgia managed?  
   A:
   - Reassurance (most effective); painless nodularity needs nothing
   - Supportive bra; less caffeine/fat
   - Topical NSAIDs; review OCP/HRT
   - Danazol, tamoxifen 10 mg (specialist, VTE risk); diuretics ineffective
   [pp. 11, 17] — media: BBD02_p11.png, BBD02_p17.png

64. Q: What causes extramammary chest pain and how is it treated?  
   A:
   - Tietze's (costochondritis); referred: cervical radiculopathy, cardiac, gallbladder, lung
   - Treat cause; NSAIDs; local steroid + anaesthetic injection for Tietze's
   [p. 11] — media: BBD02_p11.png

65. Q: What is Mondor's disease?  
   A:
   - Superficial thrombophlebitis of breast vein: pain, palpable cord, skin dimpling
   - Self-limiting 4–8 weeks; NSAIDs
   - Mimics cancer: reassure; mammography if ≥35
   [pp. 11, 12, 17] — media: BBD02_p11.png, BBD02_p12.png, BBD02_p17.png

66. Q: What is fibrocystic change and how does it present?  
   A:
   - Cysts, nodularity, stromal proliferation, epithelial hyperplasia; exaggerated hormone response
   - Premenstrual mastalgia, dense nodularity, tender macrocysts, non-bloody discharge
   - No cancer link unless atypia
   [p. 12] — media: BBD02_p12.png

67. Q: What is duct ectasia and how is it treated?  
   A:
   - Dilated subareolar major ducts; multiparous, 50–60; mimics carcinoma
   - Cheesy, toothpaste discharge, multiple ducts, bilateral; green multi-duct usually physiological
   - Reassure; Hadfield's total duct excision if troublesome or cancer possible
   [pp. 12, 13] — media: BBD02_p12.png, BBD02_p13.png

68. Q: What are the theories linking duct ectasia and periductal mastitis?  
   A:
   - Haagensen: ectasia → stagnation → ulceration → fatty acid leak → inflammation, fibrosis → retraction
   - Alternative: periductal mastitis first → dilatation
   - Current: separate conditions
   [pp. 12, 13] — media: BBD02_p12.png, BBD02_p13.png

69. Q: How do you distinguish duct ectasia from periductal mastitis?  
   A:
   - Ectasia: older, involution, thick multi-duct discharge, retraction
   - Periductal mastitis: younger, smoking/bacteria, purulent, abscess/fistula
   - Slit-like retraction benign; circumferential, new, progressive → carcinoma
   [pp. 12, 13, 14] — media: BBD02_p12.png, BBD02_p13.png, BBD02_p14.png

70. Q: How do breast cysts present and what defines a simple cyst?  
   A:
   - Age 35–50; non-integrated involution
   - Smooth, tense, sudden lump, single/multiple, uni/bilateral
   - Simple: anechoic, thin wall, posterior enhancement
   - Debris, septations, solid part → assess
   [pp. 14, 17] — media: BBD02_p14.png, BBD02_p17.png

71. Q: How are breast cysts managed, and when is a cyst suspicious?  
   A:
   - Asymptomatic simple → reassure; symptomatic → US-guided aspiration
   - Resolves + concordant → done; recurrence alone → re-aspirate, not surgery
   - Cytology only if bloody/suspicious
   - Suspicious (bloody fluid, rapid refill, residual/solid mass) → core/excision biopsy
   [pp. 14, 15, 17] — media: BBD02_p14.png, BBD02_p15.png, BBD02_p17.png

72. Q: What is a galactocele and how is it treated?  
   A:
   - Solitary milk cyst from lactation; wall calcifies with time
   - Aspiration treats and confirms
   - Recurrent/large/complicated → repeat aspiration or excision
   [p. 16] — media: BBD02_p16.png

73. Q: What is fat necrosis, and how is it diagnosed and treated?  
   A:
   - Trauma (often forgotten), surgery (reduction, reconstruction, lipofilling), radiotherapy → fat death, fibrosis
   - Mimics cancer (hard mass, retraction, calcification); oil cysts characteristic
   - Core biopsy if unsure: lipid-laden macrophages
   - No treatment once confirmed
   [pp. 16, 17] — media: BBD02_p16.png, BBD02_p17.png

74. Q: Which nipple discharges are physiological or galactorrhoea, and their causes?  
   A:
   - Physiological: bilateral, multi-duct, expressed only, milky/clear/green; normal, ectasia, fibrocystic
   - Galactorrhoea: bilateral, milky, spontaneous; pregnancy, prolactinoma, antipsychotics/metoclopramide, hypothyroidism
   [p. 16] — media: BBD02_p16.png

75. Q: What are pathological and purulent nipple discharge, and how is pathological discharge managed?  
   A:
   - Pathological: unilateral, single-duct, spontaneous, bloody/serous ± lump; papilloma, DCIS/cancer
   - Purulent: periductal mastitis, abscess
   - Triple assessment → microdochectomy or total duct excision
   [pp. 16, 17] — media: BBD02_p16.png, BBD02_p17.png
